4  Finding and Evaluating Data Sources

Before you can communicate with data, you need data worth communicating from.

This sounds obvious, but it is one of the easiest places to make a mistake. A polished chart can hide a weak source. A fluent paragraph can overstate what a dataset actually measures. An AI tool can suggest a dataset that sounds real but does not exist, or summarize a source in a way that skips the most important limitation.

In this chapter, we focus on how to find and evaluate data sources before building a memo, graph, dashboard, or report. The goal is not to find the largest dataset. The goal is to find a source that fits the question, audience, claim, and ethical responsibilities of the communication task.

4.1 Why source choice matters

A data source shapes what can be claimed.

Suppose you want to communicate about student success. Different sources support different claims:

Source What It Might Support What It May Not Support
Tutoring center visit logs Patterns in tutoring use Whether tutoring caused better grades
End-of-course survey Student perceptions Actual learning gains
Registrar course records Enrollment, grades, completion Why students struggled or succeeded
Interviews with students Lived experiences and explanations Campus-wide percentages
National education dataset Broad comparisons across institutions Details about one local program

The source does not just provide numbers. It defines the population, time frame, variables, categories, and limits of the evidence.

ExampleExample 4.1: Same topic, different source

Topic: student food insecurity.

A campus survey may help answer:

What percentage of surveyed students reported worrying about food access this semester?

A dining hall transaction dataset may help answer:

How often are meal plan swipes used during each week of the semester?

A local food pantry record may help answer:

How many pantry visits occurred each month?

These are related questions, but they are not interchangeable. The survey, dining hall data, and pantry records measure different things.

4.2 Start with a source-seeking question

Do not start with “I need a dataset about education” or “I need data about health.” Those topics are too broad.

Start with a source-seeking question:

  1. Who or what should the data describe?
  2. What measure do I need?
  3. What time period matters?
  4. What level of detail matters?
  5. What comparison or context do I need?
  6. What kind of claim am I hoping to make?

For example:

Broad Topic Better Source-Seeking Question
College enrollment What public source reports undergraduate enrollment by institution and year?
Local employment What official source reports unemployment rates by county and month?
Public health What source reports county-level health outcomes with definitions and collection notes?
Student support What campus source records tutoring visits by week and course?

A source-seeking question makes the search more efficient because it names the data structure you need.

4.3 What counts as a data source?

A data source is not only a spreadsheet. It is the full package of materials that explain where the data came from, what the rows and columns mean, and how the data may be used.

A usable data source often includes:

  • the data file or table;
  • documentation or metadata;
  • a data dictionary or codebook;
  • methodology notes;
  • date of collection or publication;
  • source owner or organization;
  • license or terms of use;
  • update schedule;
  • contact information or citation guidance.
NoteData file is not the whole source

If you download only a .csv file and ignore the documentation, you may not know what the variables mean, what population is included, how missing values are coded, or whether the data may be reused.

4.4 Documentation, metadata, and data dictionaries

Three words come up often when evaluating data sources: documentation, metadata, and data dictionary.

Term What It Usually Means Why It Matters
Documentation General explanation of the dataset, collection process, scope, and use Helps you understand what the dataset can and cannot support
Metadata Information about the dataset, such as title, owner, date, format, license, and update schedule Helps you cite, track, and judge the source
Data dictionary A list of variables, definitions, units, categories, and coding rules Helps you interpret columns correctly

For this course, do not worry about memorizing technical distinctions. The practical question is:

Can I explain where the data came from, what it means, and what limits it has?

If the answer is no, the source is not ready for communication.

4.5 Evaluating a source

Use the following checks before building a claim from a source.

Relevance

Relevance asks whether the source actually fits the question.

Ask:

  • Does the source describe the group, place, time, or process I care about?
  • Does it include the measure I need?
  • Does it include the comparison I want to make?
  • Does the level of detail fit my audience?

Low relevance creates weak claims. A national dataset may not answer a local campus question. A one-time survey may not answer a trend question. A count may not answer a rate question.

Authority

Authority asks who created or maintains the data.

Ask:

  • What organization produced the source?
  • Does the organization have a clear role or expertise related to the data?
  • Is the source original, or did it copy data from somewhere else?
  • Is there contact information, citation guidance, or documentation?

Authority does not mean the source is perfect. Official sources can still have limits. But source ownership helps you judge credibility and responsibility.

Collection method

Collection method asks how the data were created.

Ask:

  • Was the data collected through a survey, administrative record, sensor, transaction system, observation, experiment, or web scrape?
  • Who was included?
  • Who may have been missed?
  • Were responses required or voluntary?
  • Were definitions consistent over time?

The collection method tells you what kind of evidence the data are.

Definitions and measurement

Definitions ask what the variables mean.

Ask:

  • How is each key variable defined?
  • What unit is used?
  • Are categories mutually exclusive?
  • Are missing values coded as blanks, zeros, NA, 999, or something else?
  • Did the definition change over time?

A variable name is not enough. A column called completion might mean course completion, application completion, survey completion, or program completion.

Coverage

Coverage asks who or what appears in the data.

Ask:

  • What population is included?
  • What population is excluded?
  • Are there geographic limits?
  • Are there time limits?
  • Are small groups combined, suppressed, or missing?

Coverage matters because a source can be accurate for the population it includes and still misleading if used for a different population.

Timeliness

Timeliness asks whether the data are current enough for the communication task.

Ask:

  • When were the data collected?
  • When were the data published?
  • How often are they updated?
  • Is there a lag between the event and the published data?
  • Does the audience need the latest available data or a stable historical record?

Recent is not always better. For some questions, a complete older dataset may be more useful than a partial current one. But the audience should not be left guessing about timing.

Format and accessibility

Format asks whether you can actually use the data.

Ask:

  • Is the data available as .csv, .xlsx, API, database table, PDF, image, or webpage?
  • Can it be downloaded without copying and pasting by hand?
  • Is the file readable by R or another tool?
  • Are column names and formats consistent?
  • Is the dataset too large or complex for the assignment scope?

PDF tables and screenshots may be useful for reading, but they are often poor formats for analysis. A clean .csv or documented API is usually easier to use.

License and terms of use

License asks what you are allowed to do with the data.

Ask:

  • Is the data public domain, openly licensed, restricted, or unclear?
  • Are attribution or citation required?
  • Are there limits on redistribution?
  • Are there privacy or confidentiality rules?
  • Are there terms of use for an API or portal?
WarningPublic does not mean permissionless

Data can be visible online and still have terms of use, attribution requirements, privacy concerns, or ethical limits. Always check the source page and documentation before reusing or republishing data.

Provenance

Provenance means the history of the data: where it came from, how it moved, and how it changed.

For a course project, a provenance note should answer:

  • Where did the data come from?
  • Who created or maintains it?
  • When did you access it?
  • What version, year, or release did you use?
  • What file did you download?
  • What changes did you make before analysis?

Provenance is part of reproducibility. If someone cannot trace your source, they cannot fully evaluate your claim.

4.6 Source fit and claim strength

A data source does not support every claim equally.

Claim Type Source Needs
Descriptive Clear population, measure, time frame, and definitions
Comparative Same definitions across groups, fair comparison groups, and relevant denominators
Associational Variables measured for the same units or time periods, with attention to confounding
Causal Strong research design, not only a simple comparison
Predictive Data suitable for forecasting, with enough history and validation
Evaluative Evidence plus a stated criterion for what counts as good, bad, effective, or sufficient

The source and claim must match. If the source is a one-time survey, be careful with trend claims. If the source is administrative records, be careful with claims about attitudes or motivations. If the source is observational, be careful with causal language.

ExampleExample 4.2: Source fit

A city dashboard reports the number of pothole repair requests submitted each month.

This source may support:

Reported pothole repair requests increased from March to April.

It may not support:

Potholes increased from March to April.

The dashboard counts requests, not all potholes. The difference matters.

4.8 Example source note

ExampleExample 4.3: Draft data source note

Source title: County unemployment rate data

Source owner or organization: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics

Link or location: BLS data tools or public data API

Date accessed: August 22, 2026

Data format: Online table, downloadable data, or API

Population or coverage: Labor force data by geographic area

Time period: Monthly or annual series, depending on the selected table

Key variables or measures: Unemployment rate, labor force, employment, unemployment

Documentation available: BLS series documentation and API/data tool notes

License or terms of use: Public source; still cite BLS and follow portal guidance

Possible audience: Local policymakers or community members

Possible claim: The county unemployment rate changed from one month to another.

Important limitation: Month-to-month changes may need seasonal context, revision notes, and comparison to state or national patterns.

AI use or verification note: AI could help draft plain-language explanations, but source links, definitions, and the final claim must be checked against BLS documentation.

4.9 Using AI to find and evaluate sources

AI can help with source discovery, but it should not be trusted as the source itself.

Useful AI tasks:

  • brainstorm search terms;
  • suggest possible official agencies or data portals;
  • summarize documentation you provide;
  • identify variables that may matter for a question;
  • draft a source note from information you verified;
  • suggest questions to ask about a dataset.

Risky AI tasks:

  • asking AI to invent a dataset;
  • trusting a link without opening it;
  • accepting a license summary without checking the source page;
  • asking AI to decide whether a dataset proves a causal claim;
  • pasting private or identifiable data into an unapproved tool.
TipAI source rule

Use AI to help you search and think. Do not cite AI as the data source. Cite the dataset, organization, documentation, or publication that you actually inspected.

4.10 Common problems to watch for

These problems are common enough that you should check for them before writing a claim.

Problem Why It Matters
The source has data but no documentation You may not know what the variables mean
The source measures a proxy Your claim may name something the data do not directly measure
The time period is unclear The audience cannot interpret timing or trend
The denominator is missing Counts may look large or small without context
The data are aggregated Group-level data may not support individual-level claims
Categories changed over time Trend comparisons may be unfair
Missing values are not explained Summaries may be biased or incomplete
The license is unclear Reuse may be restricted
The file was modified before you received it Provenance may be incomplete

Finding one of these problems does not always mean you cannot use the source. It means your claim and caveat need to reflect the limit.

4.11 Public data sources

Public data portals can be useful starting points. They are not a substitute for source evaluation.

Examples of official public data portals include:

  • Data.gov, the U.S. government’s open data catalog.
  • data.census.gov, the U.S. Census Bureau’s data platform.
  • BLS data tools and BLS Public Data API, which provide access to Bureau of Labor Statistics data.
  • NCES IPEDS Use the Data, which provides access to postsecondary education data submitted to NCES.

When using any portal, move from the portal record to the documentation. The search result is not enough.

4.12 Practical workflow

Use this workflow before starting a data memo:

  1. Write a source-seeking question.
  2. Search likely source owners and public portals.
  3. Open the source page, not only a search result.
  4. Download or bookmark the documentation.
  5. Identify the population, time period, variables, and format.
  6. Check license, terms of use, and citation guidance.
  7. Write a data source note.
  8. Draft only claims that the source can support.
  9. Record any AI help and verification.

This workflow is slower than grabbing the first spreadsheet you find. It is also how you avoid building a polished message on weak evidence.

4.13 Recap

Keyword Definition
Data source The data file plus the documentation, metadata, ownership, license, and context needed to understand it.
Documentation Explanation of how the data were collected, what they include, and how they should be interpreted.
Metadata Information about the dataset, such as owner, title, date, format, license, and update schedule.
Data dictionary A guide to variables, definitions, units, categories, and codes.
Coverage The people, places, events, time periods, or units included in the data.
License The rules or permissions for using, sharing, or republishing the data.
Provenance The history of where the data came from, when it was accessed, and how it changed.
Source fit The match between the data source and the claim, audience, and communication task.

TipSolutions

  1. A data file alone usually does not explain where the data came from, what population is included, how variables are defined, how missing values are coded, what time period is covered, or what use restrictions apply. Without that context, it is easy to misinterpret the source.

  2. A data dictionary explains variables: names, definitions, units, categories, and codes. Metadata describes the dataset as a whole: title, owner, date, format, license, update schedule, and other source information.

  3. The dashboard counts repair requests, not all potholes. Requests can change because of reporting behavior, outreach, weather, staffing, or app use. “Repair requests increased” fits the source better than “potholes increased.”

  4. Answers will vary. Good items include source title, owner, link, date accessed, data format, population or coverage, time period, key variables, documentation, license, possible claim, limitation, and AI verification note.

  5. Public visibility does not automatically mean unrestricted reuse. The source may require attribution, limit redistribution, restrict commercial use, include privacy rules, or have terms of use for an API or portal.

  6. AI could help brainstorm search terms, identify likely source owners, summarize documentation you provide, or draft questions to ask about a dataset. You must still verify that the source exists, open the source page, read the documentation, check license or terms, and make sure the source supports the claim.
